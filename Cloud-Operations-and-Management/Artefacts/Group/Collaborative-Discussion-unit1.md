## Initial Post

Amazon Web Services (AWS) continues to retain market leadership, offering the highest number of availability zones (63) and serving major global clients such as Netflix, Facebook, and BMW (Rachita et al., 2025, p. 40). Google Cloud (GC) by contrast, maintains the lowest entry level pricing, at approximately 0.05$ per small instance, and demonstrates greater strength in artificial intelligence and machine learning workloads.

Regarding flexibility, AWS provides over 750 instance types with detailed configuration options, whereas GC supports both predefined and customisable machine types with adjustable CPU and memory. Although both platforms offer per-second billing, GC further applies sustained-use discounts automatically, enhancing its cost adaptability.

In terms of pricing, AWS proves cost efficient for steady, lightweight workloads through its 750 hour free tier, while GC remains economical for experimental and AI-driven tasks owing to its Always Free tier and flexible discount structure.

The two providers also diverge notably in artificial intelligence and machine learning. GC is recognised as particularly strong in AI and ML, and its low cost, always free resources support iterative model development, which makes it attractive to startups and research oriented teams (Rachita et al., 2025, pp. 40, 43). AWS, by contrast, is investing heavily in AI/ML through managed services such as Amazon SageMaker and in quantum computing through Amazon Braket, positioning it for advanced, enterprise-scale workloads (Rachita et al., 2025, p. 43). Consequently, GC tends to suit experimental, data-driven AI development, whereas AWS favours large scale, production grade deployment.

Regarding practical use cases, the source recommends AWS for small scale applications, academic projects, and early-stage startups requiring predictable, low cost compute. GC, conversely, is better suited to development environments, data intensive applications, and AI/ML workloads, as its pricing model favours innovation, testing, and rapid prototyping (Rachita et al., 2025, pp. 42–43).

## Peer Response 1

Thank you for your comparison. I found your point about Google Cloud's flexibility particularly interesting, especially the use of custom machine types and automatic sustained-use discounts. This can make cost management easier for organisations that are still experimenting with workload size and resource requirements.

One additional factor worth considering is vendor lock-in. AWS offers a very broad ecosystem and a large number of services, which is a major strength, but organisations that become heavily dependent on proprietary services may find future migration more complex. Google Cloud can also create this dependency, although its strong support for Kubernetes and container-based workloads may improve portability in some cloud-native environments (Gupta, 2024).

I also agree with your distinction between experimental AI workloads and large-scale production use. In practice, the final decision should probably consider not only price and technical capability but also long-term portability, staff expertise, governance and the organisation's wider cloud strategy.

Reference: Gupta, D. (2024) The Cloud Computing Journey. Birmingham: Packt Publishing.
